# Story craft: how to make an episode people think about at night
Read before writing ANY episode. This overrides "tell the events in order". The owner's verdict on Ep 2 v2: "boring". This file is the fix.

## 1. Why Ep 2 was boring (honest diagnosis)
1. **The narrator lists actions.** "You run. Reyes slams the door. You jam the blocks." That's a police report. Pictures should show actions; the voice should carry feelings and thoughts.
2. **Ollie is a prop.** He never says, does or wants anything. You can't grieve a dinosaur-shaped object.
3. **The twist arrives as data.** A dot on a map and CCTV stills: cold, distant, second-hand. The viewer learns it; they don't *feel* it.
4. **No love before the loss.** There's no warm beat before he vanishes, so the loss costs nothing.
5. **The dilemma is logistics.** Plank vs ladder. It doesn't ask "who are you?"; it asks "which exit?".
6. **Nothing is left open.** Every question gets answered on screen, so nothing follows the viewer to bed.

## 2. The rules that fix it
1. **One relationship per episode, with one tiny ritual.** Give the loved one a specific, repeatable thing (a gesture, a phrase, a promise) set up early in a warm moment. At the worst moment the ritual comes back, twisted. *That* is what haunts people, not the monster.
2. **Split-second, then slow.** The life-changing moment happens in under a second (a blink, a hand slipping, a door). Then we replay that second in slow motion, frame by frame, so the viewer lives it twice.
3. **Make the viewer guilty.** They voted. Say it: "You chose this." The best episodes make A and B *both* betray something, so commenters argue.
4. **The narrator speaks inside your head.** 50% of lines are sensation and thought ("His hand is warm. It shouldn't be."), not events. Events are shown.
5. **Show the twist through a person, not a screen.** The reveal happens in a face, a hand, a voice, an object, close and physical. Maps and CCTV only as confirmation, never as the punch.
6. **One quiet image at the end.** Before the vote card: something small and still (a toy on gravel, a hand unclenching, three taps on a radio). Loud endings are forgotten; quiet ones linger.
7. **Leave one question unanswered, on purpose.** A single line that reframes everything and is never explained ("He wasn't sleepwalking. He was walking *away* from you."). That's the thought people have at 2 AM, and it's what they comment.
8. **Every side character gets one human detail** (as in-world text, since only the narrator is voiced): Maya counting heartbeats on her watch, Reyes's wedding ring, DJ's cracked phone with a photo of his mom.
9. **Cause and effect still rules** (owner rule 11 in CLAUDE.md), but compress actions into 1-2 s shots and spend the saved time on emotional beats.
10. **Rhythm:** fast, fast, fast, then one held breath (1.5-2 s, almost silent) right before the gut punch.

## 3. Pre-writing checklist (answer these before any script)
- What is the ritual, and where is it set up warm?
- What is the split-second moment? How do we replay it?
- What does each vote option betray?
- What is the last quiet image?
- What one line will people still be thinking about at 2 AM?
- Which narrator lines are thoughts/feelings (aim for half)?

## 4. Notes on research (honest)
Searches on short-form horror craft mostly returned blogs and reviews, not studies. The consistent themes were implication over explanation, a strong ending that leaves unease or an unanswered question, and moral dilemmas that withhold resolution so the question stays with the viewer. The rules above combine those themes with standard screenwriting practice. They are craft judgment, not proven data. Real proof is your retention graphs and comments.

Fact check: "microsleeps last a fraction of a second up to ~30 s" appears mostly in secondary/popular sources; sleep research uses 5/10/30-second scoring thresholds rather than a hard 30 s maximum. Safe on-screen wording: "MICROSLEEP: SECONDS LONG. YOU WON'T KNOW IT HAPPENED."
